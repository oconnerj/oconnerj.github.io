Video games are truly amazing little environments. They're unique in that they combine the visual and auditory storytelling of movies with the visceral, personal stories created by your own experiences within the simulated world. Some games include the sprawling narratives of books, and others inspire the fierce competition inherent to sports. While any sort of content can be cross-media like this, video games are the only place you can partake in such a wide variety of them all at once.

Games, at their fundamental level, are already a sort of instanced storytelling; no two games of baseball have ever been played exactly the same way, for example. The participants have a completely bespoke instance of that game which only they can say they **experienced**. Over time, as video games evolved, that experiential story began to merge with more art forms. At first, they only really excelled at _ludonarrative_ <small>(the intersection between the gameplay and narrative)</small>, but it didn't take long for video games to be a medium through which one can experience legitimate, serious art. The ludic component is its own form of art.

In fact, that component is so nuanced that game designers have been expressing it in thousands of different ways since the inception of video games. As I love to do, though, today we're going to boil decades of rich history down into two specific points of conversation: linearity vs. discovery.

First, apologies if this section gets a little "high-minded" or artsy-fartsy. Understanding video games as art is an important pillar of being able to discuss them intelligently, even when you're talking about something as straightforward as the tradeoffs between linearity and open-ended game design.

Video games are art. Every component of them is art in some way. If Duchamp's _Fountain_ can be art because of the context and experience it's viewed within, so too can the context and experience of playing a video game be art. To put it another way, if you're willing to say that the intentionally-designed evocativeness of avant garde works is art, you must also admit that game design is art.

I think most people would agree that these parts of a video game are art:

- The meticulously painted textures
- The intricately detailed 3D models
- The balanced and evocative sound effects
- The carefully composed and dramatically orchestrated music
- The deliberate and imaginative writing
- The character and emotion of the voice acting and motion capture performances

Why, then, are the intentionality, pacing, mechanics, and flow of game design not also art? **They are**. I mean, so are the optimizations, algorithms, idioms, and logical structures of the programming, but "programming as art" is a different topic for another time.

## The Design Space

If you've spent any time reading about game design, you've probably heard the term _design space_ before. It's pretty easy to understand that on the surface level - it's the, uh, area within which a designer designs. But perhaps it's easier to discuss the concept as a metaphor instead. The **design space** is to a game designer what a **canvas** is to a painter. It's the surface upon which the artist will express themselves.

Just like a canvas, a game's design space might take several forms and a designer might use several kinds of tools to paint on it. Often, the shape of the design space is defined by the technology used in the making of the game, the game's budget and scope, and of course the designers' vision for what the gameplay will be. It's also molded by the mindset of the designers. What principles do they hold dear? What philosophies do they champion?

The canvas of Half-Life 2 is dramatically different from that of something like, say, Minecraft. Both are _technically_ 3D games in a first-person perspective with actions bound to left and right click on the mouse (or left/right trigger on controller) that depend on the avatar's current equipment. But that's like saying that technically a paint roller is a type of paint brush. No - while there are some similarities shared by these games, in practice the design space could hardly be more different.

For example, HL2 has a fundamentally opposed set of core philosophies to Minecraft. Both games want to make sure a new player has a good time, but HL2 (and Half-Life before it) work hard to craft your entire experience almost exactly as intended. The designers at Valve placed a _very strong_ emphasis on the intentionality of their gameplay cycle and flow. As a result, the game is famously linear. You experience the same beats at roughly the same time every time you play through it. Enemies are scripted to appear in the same places, and hard-coded to provide certain orchestrated set piece moments at exactly the right time. Their combat AI is configured such that they won't overwhelm you, but they also won't take it _too_ easy on you. Each piece of the game - every little Lego block it's made from - has been polished and sanded down by rigorous playtesting to fit together as perfectly as was possible in 2004. You still affect the experience as a player, but only within the tight confines of the parameters set by the design's intent.

Minecraft, on the other hand, uses the "crutch" (I use that term very loosely here) of on-screen popup tutorials to teach the player about its gameplay. "Punch a tree," it says. "Make a pickaxe. Survive through your first night." After that? It lets you loose in a completely directionless world where your only real objective is to survive. The world itself changes shape meaningfully every time you start a new game. Entire feature sets of the game, whole swaths of content, might be missed by a player during their first run. Some players may not even engage with the survival aspect of the gameplay at all and instead treat it like a literal sandbox, building up their little block world in a way which pleases them. The philosophy behind Minecraft wants to give you tools, challenges, and let you find the fun. The Half-Life principles want to give you tailored experiences more like a theme park ride.

## Throwing a Wrench in the Metal Gears

I've obviously cherry-picked two diametrically opposed games to demonstrate a point. One game's design space centers almost entirely around ways to guide you linearly into the next tailored moment. The other's instead opens up an entire world for you to discover at your own pace, using a toolset the game drops on you more-or-less without fanfare. Now let's look at a game series which somehow manages to merge these two philosophies into one: Metal Gear Solid.

If you imagine the plot of a linear video game as a literal line graph, where each major beat is a point on the line, the first Metal Gear Solid is 100% committed to the plot being a line. However, it toys a little bit with the order of those points. You have a few options that affect the plot in both minor and significant ways. Later games go harder on experimenting in this space, but we'll forego those for now since... well, they're _complicated_ games to discuss from a design perspective.

The gameplay, however, is much more open-ended than Half-Life. It has its own tutorializing, of course, clumsily crammed into the plot in a very fourth-wall breaking sort of way. But instead of moving you between set pieces directly, MGS drops you into gameplay puzzles which have multiple solutions and many approaches. Like Minecraft from the example earlier, it gives you tools, challenges, and then lets you find the fun. Unlike Minecraft, however, it does occasionally take the reins back from you to lead you from one puzzle to the next while advancing the linear plot.

Now, one could certainly argue that Half-Life and HL2 work the same way. They drop you into set pieces and combat puzzles and let you find your fun. However, the degree of freedom is very different. One could even argue that it's a thematic through-line of Half-Life's plot that you, the player, have relatively little agency in how the game plays out. You're [on a rail](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/On_A_Rail), so to speak.
