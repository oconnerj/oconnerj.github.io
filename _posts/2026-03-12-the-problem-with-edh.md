I've been playing Magic: The Gathering off-and-on since I was a kid. I remember my family cracking packs at my grandma's house during Christmas of 1994 (or thereabouts), and the first set they let me play was Ice Age in 1995. From then until about 2007, I played semi-regularly with my parents and my friends. When I got older, I started going to local tournaments and eventually started keeping up with the metagame. I got sweaty about it, for a bit.

Of course, that all ended one fateful night when I took my new deck I spent a bunch of money on to a Friday Night Magic event. This was around the time of the Ravnica/Time Spiral Standard format. I waltzed in with my expensive new toy and proceeded to get absolutely crushed by some arrogant little kid with a pre-constructed deck. In my next match, I got locked out of the game by a control deck. I basically didn't even get to play. Embarrassingly, I was so pissed off that I dropped out and just... went to go sit in the car while my friends kept playing. I quit Magic after that, and didn't play again (barring a couple of random drafts at a friend's store) for almost 15 years.

Now, the point of this story isn't to shame my teenage self for being a poor loser, although he totally was. It's also not to try and garner artificial credibility as some kind of "Magic boomer" pining for the good ol' days. It's simply to establish my background with the game. My history with it probably mirrors how a lot of folks played back then: I started off playing "kitchen table" Magic, dabbled in some local competitive events, and then dropped off after one of Magic's many [quit moments](https://youtu.be/ZhQ_jKFnFSU?si=jZ0ViBc6ulFH_WII).

Commander, or [Elder Dragon](https://scryfall.com/search?q=f%3Aedh+t%3Alegendary+t%3Aelder+t%3Adragon+prefer%3Aoldest&unique=cards&as=grid&order=released&dir=asc) Highlander as it's called if you sometimes pull a muscle when you get out of bed, wasn't even on my radar back then. Retroactively, I know it existed. Hell, Wizards of the Coast was apparently publishing first party products for it as far back as 2011. However, I missed out on its popularity explosion. I quit before Planeswalkers were a card type! How was I supposed to know about this new (to me) community-led (at the time) format which promised to bring the fun of those bumbling, awkward kitchen table decks back to the spotlight?

## The Commander Dominance

There was a time when the overwhelming majority of Magic players didn't play Standard. They also didn't play Commander, Modern, Legacy, or whatever the contemporary formats were. They just... played Magic.

My parents were like this, too. They didn't know anything about formats, or what sets were legal in them, or which cards were banned. They just made decks with the cards they had. They understood that the point of the game was to win, and to win you need to deplete the other players' life points to 0. They had a rough understanding of the basic rules, but probably couldn't resolve trample damage correctly. Banding? Please.

So, my dad would build decks that he thought were cool, usually black and blue decks with lots of Terrors and Counterspells. My mom would do the same, almost always making white decks full of angels or griffins and anthem effects. My dad won most often, but my mom would always win if he didn't draw well enough to kill her Serra Angels. I almost never won... but I was literally like 8 years old. Cut me a break.

That said, there was always a sort of casual understanding in these informal 3-player games. **We were playing to win**. Keep this in mind; it's going to come up later.

Given the prominence of kitchen table Magic in its early days - and, [according to WotC, even now](https://markrosewater.tumblr.com/post/733930737497145344/you-often-have-said-that-the-most-popular-format) - it's no surprise to me that Commander took off like it did. As stated multiple times by its early shepherds, Commander/EDH was always intended to be a way to play Magic where you could slap together some fun garbage and play the game with your friends and family. It's only slightly more structured than how my parents and I used to play:

1. It's a **singleton** format, so instead of allowing up to four of the most powerful cards you can dig up, you just get one of each.
    - This is critical in making sure other players have a harder time out-spending you financially to win, among other benefits.
2. It's a **100 card** format, so combined with the above, your ability to build reliable engines is dramatically reduced.
    - Crucially, this provides replayability and variance beyond the usual 60-card gameplay.
3. There is a **small ban list** of cards which don't play well in multiplayer, or abuse the other unique idiosyncrasies of Commander, or are the Power Nine (because of course).
4. Choosing a **Commander** gives you, as a deck builder, something to build around instead of just putting together a pile of your 100 best cards.
5. Similarly, forcing you to only play cards which align with your Commander's **color identity** ensures decks don't usually become five-color "good stuff soup".
    - In other words, you're forced to only play with a subset of cards of certain colors, no matter how flexible and powerful your deck's lands could be.
    - There are Commanders whose color identity includes all five colors, of course, but it's a very small subset of the overall pool.

Otherwise, go nuts. As cited by several prominent Magic content creators, Commander was the best way to turn a box of "draft chaff" <small>(leftover cards from playing in drafts or cracking packs)</small> into something playable and fun. Or, to put it another way, **it was a way to turn cards you already had into fun new decks**. Since this was something most players were already doing, Commander probably seemed like an objective upgrade to many of them. It also informally settled on a four-player pod as the baseline experience, which is already a very common threshold for casual multiplayer experiences in various mediums (board games, video games, and so on). The ingredients came together _just right_ to eventually evolve into a popular format.

## The Metagame Ruins Everything

I'm not going to sit here and pretend that those early games of Commander were perfect. I mean, I wouldn't know. I didn't play it back then. However, I'm willing to bet there were far fewer existential arguments about the **conceptual principles of the game itself**. If you're unfamiliar, in the modern Magic micro-culture, there are two very important guardrails which have materialized around Commander.

#### 1. The Rule Zero Conversation

This is supposed to be a chat among the players about the power levels of their decks, any particularly _spicy_ combos or win conditions, any potential rule-breaking or bending they might contain, and so on. The idea is that everyone goes into the game with a rough agreement about what they're getting into. Commander games can run very long, and no one wants to waste 3 hours watching someone play solitaire with their deck when you just wanted to cast big dinosaurs and go "rawr".

The success of this conversation varies wildly. If you're playing with friends, you might not even need it. You've probably spontaneously developed your own unspoken rules with them about how much money is reasonable to pump into a deck, how "fair" you want to play, and whether you're just messing around or if you're playing to win.

If you're playing with less familiar people, like random groups at your local game store or acquaintances you don't know very well, the conversation boils down to what kind of people they are. Humans are remarkably complex in their motivations, desires, needs, and relative morality. You will absolutely play Magic with people who cheat. Yes, even at Commander. You'll play with people that use all the most expensive cards because they say their favorite Magic set was "HP Masters." <small>(That is, they printed all their cards on an inkjet printer.)</small> And, of course, you'll play with perfectly reasonable people with whom you happen to disagree on the spirit of Commander. More on _that_ in a minute.

Whether this conversation actually works or not isn't the point. It has crystallized into a part of the format, for better or worse.

#### 2. The Commander Brackets System

Introduced relatively recently (early 2025), Wizards of the Coast attempted to slightly formalize the classification of decks in Commander by creating a sort of [rubric of power](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/announcements/introducing-commander-brackets-beta). The intent is to replace the informal and inaccurate measurements previously used by the community with something more standardized. This series of semi-rigid criteria tries to sort all Commander decks - which can be comprised of almost any combination of tens of thousands of unique cards - into one of five brackets.

The system brings with it a _soft_ ban list of cards called Game Changers. These are cards deemed powerful or impactful enough to only be legal at high levels of play, and then only in limited numbers except at the **highest** levels. It also codifies several of those formerly community-driven metrics which had been used in the past to evaluate deck power level:

1. Number of easy combos <small>(defined here as requiring only two cards)</small>.
2. Correlating power to the number of non-land tutors in the deck.
    - <small>(A "tutor" in Magic is a card which lets you search your deck for another specific card. These are considered powerful in Commander because they essentially act as "wild cards" in a format which otherwise attempts to _reduce_ your ability to play consistently, as explained above in the Commander Dominance section.)</small>
    - This was removed in the most recent version of the system (at time of writing), but was a prominent part of the initial release.
3. Presence of "mass land denial".
    - <small>(These are cards or combos which prevent one or more players from playing the game, basically, by removing their ability to generate mana through their lands.)</small>
4. The ability for the deck to obtain and/or chain together extra turns.
5. Number of those aforementioned Game Changers.

Perhaps most importantly, it tries to create a tier system of power levels that players can more or less agree upon. A Bracket 1 or Bracket 2 deck might be closer to the old style of "throw together whatever you've got," but Brackets 3, 4, and 5 are all varying degrees of optimized. Wizards has been careful to position this bracket-based system as a set of _guidelines_, but they lean on the prominence of the Rule Zero conversation as the ultimate arbiter of what goes in a given game.

### But Why?

If you're not entrenched in Magic's micro-culture, you might be starting to pick up on the thesis here. Didn't I spend several paragraphs earlier talking about how casual, fun, and informal Commander was supposed to be? Wasn't it supposed to be similar to those slop decks my family and I put together back in the 90s? All these rules and guidelines and structures... it all sounds an awful lot like those other formats. The competitive ones: Standard, Modern, and so on.

You're right. Increasingly, a metagame has formed around Commander. In fact, I would argue that Wizards of the Coast has _encouraged_ the creation of one. After all, by being an official body running the format and releasing a set of material rules around the way decks are to be built and games are to be played, they've formalized it. In doing so, they've made space for yet another meta: optimizing around the bracket. What's the most powerful Bracket 2 deck one can build? What are the three best Game Changers for a Simic lands commander? Does it _really_ go against the spirit of a Bracket 3 game if I win the game on turn 5, instead of turn 6?

To some degree, this isn't the fault of WotC or any of their collaborators (like the Commander Format Panel). _**Everything has a meta now**_. The widespread availability of information that comes with access to the internet has (probably) been a net positive for humanity, but it comes at a cost. I'm not going to talk here about the human cost of social media or the immeasurable damage done by right-wing ideologues and billionaires owning huge swaths of the web. That's a topic for another time. Instead we're going to focus on the loss of innocence that comes with having effortless, instantaneous access to "the best cards for your Commander deck" or "the tier 1 decks from Pro Tour Wherever".

The reason kitchen table Magic works, and why it's still the "purest" form of Magic, is simple. Largely, the people playing it are either ignorant of the meta or choose to intentionally ignore it. These players don't know that an objectively better version of their favorite 6 mana sorcery released in Aetherdrift as a mythic rare. They just play the card because it works in their deck and they like it. They don't know that the surveil lands from Murders at Karlov Manor are amazing cards with a hefty price tag. They sit in a box in the closet because they enter tapped. _"That sucks, and why would I want to put a card in my graveyard on purpose?"_

Unfortunately, these players are becoming extinct. It's hard not to break out of that innocence when there is so much Magic content and so many products being made. A simple Google search can shatter that glass permanently. Not to mention that if a player ends up really liking the game, they _want_ to learn more. They decide to watch that highly-polished [Game Knights](https://www.youtube.com/@commandcast) video with 5 million views (sponsored by Wizards of the Coast), or the interesting-sounding [Tolarian Community College](https://www.youtube.com/@TolarianCommunityCollege) video with the funny Micro Center ad. Before they know it, they've entered the micro-culture and begin to absorb it... to become part of it. If you've never watched the Folding Ideas video, [Why It's Rude to Suck at Warcraft](https://youtu.be/BKP1I7IocYU?si=BkblQaInPNyIwB7C), you should. It perfectly describes the trap created by **the meta**. It's as true for Magic as it is for World of Warcraft. The more connected we become, the harder it is to avoid it.

## Magic Has Become Commander

Wizards of the Coast has a financial incentive to sell as many cards to as many people as possible. This means it's only natural for them to target the casual players more than the established ones; to appeal to the masses and hope to get them hooked on their definitely-not-gambling scheme. It's why so much of Magic is now _Universes Beyond_ - crossovers with popular third-party IPs like Final Fantasy or Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. These are recognizable brands that have an audience outside of Magic's typical sphere. Millions of people that have played Final Fantasy have probably never tried Magic, but they might if their favorite characters are on the cards.

These lured players will never go straight into sweating it up at their local game store, playing in 1v1 Standard tournaments. No, WotC realized that the casual nature of Commander was perfect as an avenue to expand their reach while still providing a _mostly_ structured play environment. They can continue printing powerful Commander cards (remember [Nadu](https://scryfall.com/card/mh3/193/nadu-winged-wisdom)?) in new sets to keep demand high with entrenched players _**and also**_ attract the sort of crowd who plays a Spider-Man deck because they really like Spider-Man, whether it can win the game or not. It's a win/win for them.

In fact, in many ways, Magic has become Commander and vice versa. While Standard and the other 1v1 competitive formats still exist and enjoy success, Commander is now the default way to play. Just like the kitchen table Magic of old, right?

## Game Engine Friction

Remember how I said earlier that my parents and I had an understanding back in the 90s? We played Magic to win. That quaint combination of being both **a)** naive enough to build dumpster decks based on simple themes and also **b)** competitive enough to play primarily to win... that's hard to maintain. Like I said earlier, once the glass shatters, you can't put it back together. That innate understanding, though, didn't spawn from naivety.

You might imagine we were just hyper-competitive. That's certainly true to some degree. My dad was always ruthless in every video game, board game, or card game we played. He liked to win, and my mom hated to lose. **But that's not it either**.

No, I want to delve deeper. I'm going to propose that Magic's thematic design and game engine are inherently aggressive and competitive. The pillars of its design are built from a cut-throat mindset. The game only has one true victory condition: you must defeat your opponents. The sole objective presented to you by the game is to win.

### Thematic Competition

    <img src="/images/mtg_brothers_war.jpeg" />
</div>

Even thematically, from its very inception, Magic was built around 1v1 competition. It's all about two powerful wizards slinging spells and creatures at each other until one of them perishes. You can feel it in the vibes of early sets. [It's evident in the way the rulebooks presented the game and its world](https://www.scribd.com/document/432170026/Magic-the-Gathering-1994-Rulebook). The early story focus on the two brothers - Urza vs. Mishra - was as a surrogate for you vs. your opponent(s).

As the game grew beyond generic theme sets like _Arabian Nights_ and into what would eventually become an ongoing plot, these thematic through-lines diminished, but it took several years for them to disappear entirely. Even with the introduction of Planeswalker cards in 2007's _Lorwyn_, Magic's designers were very careful to preserve the game's core thematic conceit. You, the player, are still a powerful Planeswalker. You're just summoning these other Planeswalkers as temporary allies. That's why a Planeswalker's counters are called _**loyalty counters**_: because after getting damaged enough or doing enough for you, they're no longer loyal enough to stick around. They don't die, they just leave.

### Mechanical Aggression

Behind the fantasy flavor of spell-slinging Planeswalkers, though, lies a game engine. The philosophy behind this engine was influencing its mechanics even during its earliest iterations. Let's break down a couple examples.

#### Instants

Magic has always been a game about interaction between players. Naturally, 30-something years of design iteration and player skill improvement have turned that interaction into a well-understood economy, but instants (and the now-defunct interrupts) were present from the very beginning.

The equivalent of Magic's instant-speed actions aren't actually all that common in games. Think about most other games you've played: card games, tabletop games, board games, and even most competitive video games that aren't real-time simulations. Sometimes, the game will permit you a set of "reactions" you can take based on triggers. Your opponents either know the trigger conditions, or they know that _some kind of condition_ exists. Rarely, it makes actions available that can be played "at any time" and affect the game state immediately, but these are often heavily restricted. The game may only feature a couple of them in the entire scenario, and they're usually very powerful. For them to be secret information? Even more unusual.

Magic takes the idea much further. These cards are integral to the game. Certain decks profit immensely from acting almost entirely on _another player's turn_. You're encouraged to do this, because you shorten the window of time during which your resources (mana, creatures, etc.) are expended. The game design begs you to wait until the last possible second to make your play; you put yourself at a disadvantage otherwise. As a competitive player, it can feel great to master this! You've minimized your weaknesses and maximized your strengths, giving you a leg up on your opponent. 

If you're both playing at that level, a whole new level of intrigue develops. Now you're not playing cards anymore so much as you're playing each other. An entire language develops around these mind games - for example, the phrase _"you've got to make them have it"_ refers to the idea that you can't play the game in fear of what your opponent _might_ have in their hand. Instead, you have to make calculated bets based on your understanding of the **metagame** (what their deck might contain), **public knowledge** (cards in the graveyard or exile they've already spent), and that player's **disposition** (how they might act or react). Sometimes you have to purposefully make risky plays because you need to force the opponent to reveal more information.

Competently utilizing this, however, necessitates a pretty deep understanding of the rules. When can I play instants? What is the stack? When do triggered abilities go on the stack? What is priority? Who has it right now? Why can't I stop this creature's triggered ability from firing when it enters the battlefield? Why can't I keep my opponent from activating their Planeswalker's +1 ability? Why can't I respond to them tapping their lands to get ready to cast a spell?

The comprehension and proper usage of instant-speed interaction is probably one of the biggest differentiators between a novice and an expert player. Newer players are going to get very frustrated when they try to play the game and you take advantage of these mechanics to shut them down. Countering their spells is one thing, but how about casting Lightning Bolt in response to them targeting their creature with an Aura? The creature dies; both it and the Aura go to the graveyard. This is known as a "two-for-one" - your **one** spell cost your opponent **two** game objects. How could they even know that was something they should watch for? Why even play these cards if the opponent can (hopefully metaphorically) slap them out of your hand and laugh at you?

Compare Magic in this way to Hearthstone, Blizzard's Warcraft-themed digital trading card game. Now, a disclaimer: I haven't played it in a _long time_ so I can't be sure this still holds true. However, while Magic and Hearthstone are superficially similar, their design pillars are very different. There is no instant-speed interaction in Hearthstone. It's played entirely "on the board"; that is, game pieces which can interact with the game state are laid out onto the playfield and visible to both players. This doesn't necessarily make it less of a tactical or strategic game, but it does absolutely change the feeling of playing it. Its design isn't as _aggressive_ as Magic's.

#### Lands

Magic's design also takes a very cavalier attitude toward players getting completely shut out of the game by randomness. Random elements are a huge part of most games, especially card games. Part of the appeal is that no two games are likely to play out exactly the same. However, in Magic, the peaks and valleys of this randomness are _steep_. This high-variance, high-impact facet of its design contributes to its inherent competitiveness. You might argue otherwise - that a competitive game should strive to be more fair - but that's the thing. It views fairness in the most absolute way. All players are subject to the same conditions, and have the same responsibility to mitigate their risk.

One of the most common and painful manifestations of this factor is getting a poor distribution of lands during the game. Called either "mana screw" or "mana flood" (too few or too many lands, respectively), a player can randomly be left unable to play effectively. Putting aside a few edge cases, lands are vital to a player's ability to play their cards. Almost every card in the game has a mana cost, and activated abilities often do too. So, you need a steady flow of mana to keep playing cards and taking game actions. Additionally, because the game assumes you'll continue to draw lands as the game progresses, your game-ending threats tend to cost more mana. You need to have enough mana later in the game to cast your win conditions. Not having enough of it, when you need it, leaves you sitting with a hand full of fun things you can't afford to do.

On the flip side, you also need to draw cards that have an impact. Few decks can win the game after drawing lands four turns in a row. They just... run out of stuff to do. The space between mana screw and mana flood is where a majority of matches operate, and I'd wager most have a healthy variance here which, on average, creates exciting gameplay for all parties. But, Magic is happy to let you have a very bad time.

A player is supposed to account for these factors during deck-building, and that's a great mechanic! Learning to include _enough_ lands in your deck, _but not too many_, to account for the "mana curve" <small>(the distribution of casting costs)</small>? Good idea. Doing the math to tweak your chance of casting your critical spells on time? Another mark of an expert player. But even the best players can only mitigate so much risk. They get mana flooded and screwed too, just less frequently than a novice.

Thankfully, the game does provide one built-in mechanic to help dodge these screw/flood situations after deck-building. A player can choose to mulligan their starting hand before the match begins. However, this still puts the player at a measurable, objective disadvantage due solely to bad luck. You don't get to draw a new, full hand of 7 cards. You have to pitch one of your new cards to the bottom of your library. Your opponent has 7 game pieces but now you only have 6. Still screwed after the mulligan? You can do it again... now you're down to 5 cards, and so on.

Entrenched Magic players will regale you with stories about times they've won games after taking a deep mulligan, and those are valid and exciting moments worthy of memorializing. It's also not a good idea to let players take infinite mulligans to get _just the right hand_, because that disproportionately favors combo decks who could stack their opening hand perfectly to win quickly. The point here isn't to poke holes in the rules. It's to highlight that these design pillars are aggressive and competitive by nature.

#### Boy-coded

<div style="float: right; padding: 10px 30px 10px 30px; min-width: 200px; max-width: 250px;">
<img src="/images/xtreme_90s.jpeg" />
</div>

Magic's design is full of interactions like this. These are important mechanics which are inherently _aggressive_ but absolutely part of proficient play. Magic's design pillars are highly competitive and cut-throat. The game wants you to play it like this, and will punish you if you don't. My wife has called it "boy-coded", which I think is an apt description. She loves drafting, deck-building, and admiring the card art, but kind of hates playing the game itself sometimes because of this. It's very much a product of the hyper-exaggerated _**Xtreme**_ 1990s during which it was born.

Have a look at some of the [early TV ads for Magic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fn2BMbg5yLo). Or, [this series of spots aired on MTV back in the day](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GGa_tbspGpU). Wizards of the Coast leaned into it. They knew what sort of crowd the game had attracted and chose to cultivate it. Thankfully, the marketing has toned this sort of machismo bullshit _way down_. But that same energy can't be removed from the engine without completely changing the rules. It wouldn't be the same game.

## It's You. You're The Problem

We've established that Magic has a philosophy baked into its design which encourages highly competitive and aggressive play. It's that subconscious suggestion that made my parents and I understand, back in the 90s, that Magic was a game meant to be played to win. So why, then, is so much of the discourse around Commander so _confused_? Why is the Commander Bracket System so concerned with the "feeling" of the game and managing players' expectations? Why is the metagame of Commander so concerned with the **_experience of playing it_**?

Well... it's because of you. You can't agree with others, or with the game engine itself, on what the spirit of Commander is supposed to be.

### Too Many Things to Too Many People

Commander is a **divisive** format, despite being the de facto way to play Magic. When you sit down to play a game of Standard with a single opponent at FNM, you're both entering that match with the understanding that you're playing a game, but you're going to try and win that game. That's why you're playing. The act of playing the game is intrinsically fun. Commander tries to strike an uneasy balance between that motivation - to win - and making "having fun" an explicit goal rather than an intrinsic one.

Instead of you and one opponent, you've got four people tied up in this game. Turns take longer because each player has four boards worth of game state to manage instead of two - an _exponential_ increase in complexity. Decks are bigger and life pools are larger. Inevitably, **playing Commander becomes more of a social experience**. You chat with the other players while someone is searching their library, or trying to reason about their hand, or resolving a complex stack. You're encouraged to begin every game with a friendly conversation, that aforementioned Rule Zero, wherein you're supposed to open up about your deck and its game plan a little. Really, any time you sit down with multiple other people at a table to play a game, you're more likely to treat it as a social gathering. Just ask any tabletop RPG group whose players spend a large chunk of the playtime gabbing about their day.

But things get messy once the game actually begins. The reason Wizards of the Coast created the bracket system, the reason why that Rule Zero conversation became crystallized, and the cause of all the confusion is because people want an array of different things out of their social gaming experiences. Getting a group together that all want the same thing can be challenging.

The bracket system has a place for people who play Commander to win. It's bracket 5, called CEDH (the `C` stands for competitive, I guess). CEDH is a long-standing variant of Commander/EDH wherein the only major difference in the mindset the players bring. They elect to play Commander the same way every other mainline Magic format is played. They use the best cards and strategies they can, they follow the meta, and they play to their maximum ability.

The existence of Competitive EDH implies that "normal" Commander isn't meant to be as competitive. Commander has taken on a life, for some players, as a place to goof around with the Magic game engine. Even that, though, falls on a spectrum of severity. Again, you see this in the codified guidelines of the bracket system - it tries to distill that spectrum into discrete boundaries.

- Bracket 4? You're using all the best cards, probably, but not paying too much attention to the meta decklists. 
- Bracket 3? You want to play to win but only with things you find fun, and without necessarily breaking the bank.
- Brackets 2 and 1? You're just happy to be playing Magic and slapping cardboard on the table. That's not to imply you won't try and win the game - it has to end eventually, after all - but you're not too bothered about it.

Maybe you disagree with my assessments of the individual brackets, and that's fine. It's hard to distill a spectrum of so many different expectations into only five groups. In fact, that's my point. You don't agree on what Commander is supposed to be. These sorts of disagreements form the foundation of the constantly churning discourse around the format.

### Friction Burns

This is where that _competitive_ and _aggressive_ nature inherent to Magic comes to a head. The very game engine itself is nudging you to play a certain way, but that way isn't necessarily conducive to a fun social experience. It's not _fun_, strictly speaking, to get two-for-one'd by instant speed removal. Getting bored while a combo player meticulously demonstrates their winning infinite loop isn't going to brighten up your Friday night. The best social games are designed with _wildly_ different philosophies. Their rules and interactions are built in ways that encourage fun gameplay with other players. Magic is... very much not. It's designed for you to build a deck that wins the game, and then to take advantage of its entire toolset to do so.

Because of this and the variety of social expectations players bring to the game, everyone has a different way they prefer to play. Some of these preferences are _mildly_ incompatible with others. Some are diametrically opposed.

### The Spirit of Commander

The irony of all this is everyone knows Commander is a flawed format. Even its creators willingly acknowledged this. It was described as a way to have some fun between more traditional 1v1 matches. I won't imply that folks like Sheldon Menery (the "godfather" of EDH) didn't put a lot of love and care into its development. They absolutely did. But I do strongly believe that it was never meant to grow as big as it has, nor was it meant to be the _default format_ of Magic.

It's not unreasonable, then, to say that the spirit of Commander is whatever you want it to be. Clearly, Magic itself is a fun enough game - and a timeless enough design - that despite all this friction, dissonance, and discourse around Commander, people just want to play the damn game. Getting to play with more people? That's a net positive, even if it's hard to nail down exactly what each player is looking to get out of it.

But the growing pains associated with the emphasis and codification Wizards has been placing on it? To me, that's one of the only things that _actually_ goes against the spirit of Commander.

## More Meta, More Problems

As I alluded to earlier, Magic is Commander and Commander is Magic. Wizards is highly invested in making sure it's successful, and like centralized governing bodies with a financial interest are wont to do, they have been putting structure around it so they can protect it. But EDH doesn't feel like it was ever meant to be structured. Its charm was in its lack thereof - a return to the kitchen table slop decks of the past. A way to play with cards we already had...

...oh. Yeah, I guess that's kind of a problem for them, huh?

I don't even know if the designers or bean counters at the company are doing this intentionally. I think it's probably just emergent behavior created from this sort of corporate environment. But it's hard to deny how _convenient_ it is that their actions on the format have largely served to create a meta. No one's going to blame them for printing attractive cards in new sets; that's their business model and **I want the designers and artists to get paid**. But as we established earlier, kitchen table players don't really pay attention to the meta. They're not going to know that a better card exists unless they happen to open a pack that has one in it.

So, Wizards is incentivized to do the following:

1. Create a welcoming environment that encourages new/casual players to join the meta through official events at your LGS (and, nowadays, sponsored YouTube videos showing friendly high-level Commander play).
2. Establish a structured play environment where the meta is stratified into tiers within which players can choose their comfort level.
3. Allow that meta to form. Then, let these players participate and inevitably get defeated by others who min/max within it, no matter the bracket.
4. Sell players better cards to improve their decks and compete within the meta.

There's not really anything wrong with any of this. It is, after all, the same way other Magic formats have worked since the creation of Type 2 in 1995. And hey, if you're having fun, then it's no more harmful than any other commercialized hobby. But, arguably, **it's not at all what EDH was meant to be**. You can make the same argument about the creation of Commander-exclusive products and cards legal only in Commander <small>(and I guess Legacy/Vintage)</small>. 

If the original intent of EDH was for it to be a casual format of slapped-together decks made from cards we already had, to have some less-competitive fun within a highly-competitive game, it's clearly deviated from that intent. Today, it's closer than ever before to the other first-party Magic formats. It's more akin to Modern now: it's got an expensive meta, its own dedicated product lines, a cycle of _soft_ rotation where new cards keep power-creeping out old established staples, and a fervent community of players constantly discussing, optimizing, and picking it apart. The existence of sites like EDHRec consolidates these influences. <small>(No hate to them, though. Someone was going to make a tool like it, and they seem like cool people.)</small>

Reiterating a point from earlier, some amount of this was inevitable. **Everything** has a meta. But it seems to me that the only thing Commander has left going for it to differentiate it spiritually from other formats - the casual and social factor - is starting to fray. And it's fraying because WotC can't stop sticking their fingers in the pie. Their need to protect and standardize their primary money-making format is going to smother the one thing which makes it so unique.

## Coming Apart At The Seams

I'm not making any new discoveries about Magic or Commander when I call it a social format. The [semi-official Commander format site](https://mtgcommander.net/index.php/the-philosophy-of-commander/), once operated by the now-disbanded Commander Rules Committee, is very clear about this:

> Commander is **social**. Each game is a journey the players share, where every player is considerate of the experiences of everyone involved. Magic is a competition in the same sense that all games are competitions, but whenever the act of competing comes into conflict with a social atmosphere, Commander prioritizes and protects the social atmosphere.

If you've read this far, you'll know that I disagree with this point somewhat. Games are often competitions, yes, but Magic is special in that aggressive competition is baked into its DNA. Still, the format's shepherds made it very clear. This is a social format and competition is **not** to get in the way of having a good time with people.

Gee, whatever happened to that committee?

Oh. Holy shit.

Yeah. It turns out that the toxic machismo underlying Magic is still alive and well. Like with far too many micro-cultures today, there is an underbelly of seething man-children who have been taught since the days of 4chan that they can wield devastating power through anonymity and hate. While the Magic brand and many of its most prolific personalities are pro-trans, openly gay, or transparently liberal, the view from "on the ground" (so to speak) is a mixed bag. Your LGS is probably frequented simultaneously by many loving, caring individuals who just want to play a fun card game and also a few absolute losers who try their best to spoil the experience for everyone else.

Unfortunately, because Magic is as massive as it is, there are enough of these chuds to make a serious impact on the community. Because Magic still has that undercurrent of adolescent aggression running through it, they feel at home and welcomed. And, because Wizards of the Coast wants their money, they will be allowed to participate no matter how much harm they bring.

Let me be abundantly clear for a second. I **absolutely do not** mean to imply that every Commander player who wishes the format were taken a little more seriously is a toxic asshole. You could hold the opinion that the only valid way to play is CEDH, and so long as you're cool about it, you're still welcome at my table. Sadly, there is a clear correlation between the people who thrive on and feed into the meta and those doxxing hatemongers. If you are the former and you dislike the latter, do everything you can to force them out of the community. Make them objectively unwelcome at any event, any table, any pod. There's no space in the world for people like that - make them feel like they want their targets to feel.

## YOU Are The Problem

I told you earlier the story of how I got into Magic and how I engaged with it initially. I was a try-hard, but also a sore loser. I played to win... but when I couldn't win, I quit. I was one of these toxic people, caught up in the **Xtreme** idea I was sold that playing this game made me smarter or cooler. My ego was damaged by losing because I had painted myself, in my mind, as someone who deserved to win because I was more intelligent than my opponents.

Thank god I was capable of self-reflection. It wasn't easy and it will never be "finished", but I matured emotionally. I stopped playing hyper-competitive games like League of Legends or Overwatch because I hated what they brought out of me. I think some part of me quit Magic back then, too, because I was starting to recognize my unhealthy relationship with it. I learned to appreciate _being with friends_ for its own sake, instead of treating everything as an opportunity to compete for dominance. I stopped being the kind of person who would get caught up in the excitement and yell "SORRY!" at my wife whenever we'd play that board game.

Now, I'm going to turn the mirror around to you.

Look deeply into it. Who do you see?

Some of you will see a kind person who brings joy and positivity into the world. Some of you will see what I saw back in 2007. A negative, toxic child who needs to grow up.

You see, despite everything I've just said, the real problem with Commander isn't the meta. It's not Magic's cut-throat game engine. It's not Wizards of the Coast. All of these things can be accounted for just by being an emotionally mature and responsible player.

The problem with Commander is you.
