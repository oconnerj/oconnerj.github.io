<small>(This article assumes you have at least casual familiarity with Magic: The Gathering and its popular gameplay formats. If you don't, well... you probably don't care what I have to say about the Commander format either.)</small>

I've been playing Magic: The Gathering off-and-on since I was a kid. I remember my family cracking packs of Revised at my grandma's house during Christmas of 1994 (or thereabouts), and the first set they let me play was Ice Age in 1995. From then until about 2007, I played semi-regularly with my parents and my friends. When I got older, I started going to local tournaments and eventually started keeping up with the metagame. I got sweaty about it, for a bit.

Of course, that all ended one fateful night when I took my new deck I had spent a bunch of money on to a Friday Night Magic event. This was around the time of the Ravnica/Time Spiral Standard format. I waltzed in with my expensive new toy and proceeded to get absolutely crushed by some snot-nosed kid with a pre-constructed deck. Then, I got locked out of the game by multiple control decks. I basically didn't even get to play. Embarrassingly, I was so pissed off that I dropped out and just... went to go sit in the car while my friends kept playing. I quit Magic after that, and didn't play again (barring a couple of random drafts at a friend's store) for almost 15 years.

Now, the point of this story isn't to shame myself for being a poor loser as a teenager. It's also not to try and garner artificial credibility as some kind of "Magic boomer" who liked things as they were in the good ol' days. It's simply to establish my background with the game. My history with it probably mirrors how a lot of folks played back then: I started off playing "kitchen table" Magic, dabbled in some local competitive events, and then dropped off.

Commander, or Elder Dragon Highlander as it's called if you sometimes pull a muscle when you stand up too fast, wasn't even on my radar back then. Retroactively, I know it existed. Hell, Wizards of the Coast was apparently publishing first party products for it as far back as 2011. However, I missed out on its popularity explosion. I quit before Planeswalkers were a card type. How was I supposed to know about this new community-led format which promised to bring the fun of those bumbling, awkward kitchen table decks back to the spotlight?

## The Commander Dominance

There was a time when the overwhelming majority of Magic players didn't play Standard. They also didn't play Commander, Modern, Legacy, or whatever the contemporary formats were. They just... played Magic.

My parents were like this, too. They didn't know anything about formats, or what sets were legal in them, or which cards were banned. They just made decks with the cards they had. They understood that the point of the game was to win, and to win you need to deplete all the other players' life points to 0. They had a rough understanding of the basic rules, but probably couldn't resolve trample damage correctly (as an example). Banding? Please.

So, my dad would build decks that he thought were cool, usually black and blue decks with lots of Terrors and Counterspells. My mom would do the same, almost always making white decks full of angels or griffins and anthem effects. My dad won most often, but my mom would always win if he didn't draw well enough to kill her Serra Angels. I almost never won... but I was literally like 8 years old. Cut me a break.

That said, there was always a sort of casual understanding in these informal 3-player games. **We were playing to win**. Keep this in mind; it's going to come up later.

Given the prominence of kitchen table Magic in its early days - and, according to WotC, even now - it's no surprise to me that Commander took off like it did. As stated multiple times by its early shepherds, Commander/EDH was always intended to be a way to play Magic where you could slap together some fun garbage and play the game with your friends and family. It's only slightly more structured than how my parents and I used to play:

1. It's a **singleton** format, so instead of allowing up to four of the most powerful cards you can dig up, you just get one of each.
    - This is critical in making sure other players have a harder time out-spending you financially to win, among other benefits.
2. It's a **100 card** format, so combined with the above, your ability to build reliable engines is dramatically reduced.
    - Similarly crucial, this provides replayability and variance to the game beyond the usual 60-card gameplay.
3. There is a **small ban list** of cards which don't play well in multiplayer, or abuse the other unique idiosyncrasies of Commander, or are the Power Nine (because of course).
4. Choosing a **Commander** gives you, as a deck builder, something to build around instead of just putting together a pile of your 100 best cards.

Otherwise, go nuts. As cited by several prominent Magic content creators, Commander was the best way to turn a box of "draft chaff" <small>(leftover cards from playing in drafts or cracking packs)</small> into something playable and fun. Or, to put it another way, **it was a way to turn cards you already had into fun new decks**. Since this was something most players were already doing, Commander probably seemed like an objective upgrade to many of them. It also informally settled on a four-player pod as the baseline experience, which is already a very common threshold for casual multiplayer experiences in various mediums (board games, video games, and so on).

## The Metagame Ruins Everything

I'm not going to sit here and pretend that those early games of Commander were perfect. I mean, I wouldn't know. I didn't play it back then. However, I'm willing to bet there were far fewer existential arguments about the **conceptual principles of the game itself**. If you're unfamiliar, in the modern Magic micro-culture, there are two very important guardrails which have materialized around Commander.

#### 1. The Rule Zero Conversation

This is supposed to be a chat among the players about the power levels of their decks, any potential rule-breaking or bending they might contain, and so on. The idea is that everyone goes into the game with a rough agreement about what they're getting into. Commander games can run very long, and no one wants to waste 3 hours watching someone play solitaire with their deck when you just wanted to cast big dinosaurs and go "rawr".

The success of this conversation varies wildly. If you're playing with friends, you might not even need it. You've probably spontaneously developed your own unspoken rules with them about how much money is reasonable to pump into a deck, how "fair" you want to play, and whether you're just messing around or if you're playing to win.

If you're playing with less familiar people, like random groups at your local game store or acquaintances you don't know very well, the conversation boils down to what kind of people they are. Humans are remarkably complex in their motivations, desires, needs, and relative morality. You will absolutely play Magic with people who cheat. Yes, even at Commander. You'll play with people that use all the strongest cards because they say their favorite Magic set was "Hewlett Packard Masters." <small>(That is, they printed all their cards on an inkjet printer.)</small> And, of course, you'll play with perfectly reasonable people with whom you just so happen to disagree on the spirit of Commander. More on _that_ in a minute.

Whether this conversation actually _works_ or not isn't the point. The point is that it has crystallized into a part of the format, for better or worse.

#### 2. The Commander Brackets System

Introduced relatively recently (early 2025), Wizards of the Coast attempted to slightly formalize the classification of decks in Commander by creating a sort of [rubric of power](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/announcements/introducing-commander-brackets-beta). This series of semi-rigid criteria tries to sort all Commander decks - which can be comprised of almost any combination of tens of thousands of unique cards - into one of five brackets.

This system brings with it a _soft_ ban list of cards called Game Changers - deemed powerful or impactful enough to only be legal at high levels of play, and only in limited numbers except in the **highest** levels. It also codifies several idioms around the format which had been used informally in the past to evaluate deck power level:

1. Number of easy combos <small>(defined here as requiring only two cards)</small>.
2. Correlating power to the number of non-land tutors in the deck.
    - <small>(A "tutor" in Magic is a card which lets you search your deck for another specific card. These are considered powerful in Commander because they essentially act as "wild cards" in a format which otherwise attempts to _reduce_ your ability to play consistently, as explained above in the Commander Dominance section.)</small>
    - This was removed in the most recent version of the system (at time of writing), but was a prominent part of the initial release.
3. Presence of "mass land denial".
    - <small>(These are cards or combos which prevent one or more players from playing the game, basically, by removing their ability to generate mana through their lands.)</small>
4. The ability for the deck to obtain and/or chain together extra turns.

Perhaps most importantly, it tries to create a tier system of power levels that players can more or less agree upon. A Bracket 1 or Bracket 2 deck might be closer to the old style of "throw together whatever you've got," but Brackets 3, 4, and 5 are all varying degrees of optimized. Wizards has been careful to position this bracket-based system as a set of _guidelines_, but they lean on the prominence of the Rule Zero conversation as the ultimate arbiter of what goes in a given game.

### But Why?

If you're not entrenched in Magic's micro-culture, you might be starting to pick up on the thesis here. Didn't I spend several paragraphs earlier talking about how fun and casual and informal Commander was supposed to be? Wasn't it supposed to be similar to those slop decks my family and I put together back in the 90s? All these rules and guidelines and structures... it all sounds an awful lot like those other formats - Standard, Legacy, and so on.

You're right. Increasingly, a metagame has formed around Commander. In fact, I would argue that Wizards of the Coast has _encouraged_ the creation of one. After all, by being an official body running the format and releasing a set of material rules around the way decks are to be built and games are to be played, they've formalized it. In doing so, they've made space for yet another meta: optimizing around the bracket. What's the most powerful Bracket 2 deck one can build? What are the three best Game Changers for a Simic lands commander? Does it _really_ go against the spirit of a Bracket 3 game if I win the game on turn 5 with Coalition Victory, instead of turn 6?

To some degree, this isn't the fault of WotC or any of their collaborators (like the Commander Format Panel). _**Everything has a meta now**_. The widespread availability of information that comes with access to the internet has (probably) been a net positive for humanity, but it comes at a cost. I'm not going to talk here about the human cost of social media or the immeasurable damage done by right-wing ideologues and billionaires owning huge swaths of the web. That's a topic for another time. Instead we're going to focus on the loss of innocence that comes with having effortless, instantaneous access to "the best cards for your Commander deck" or "the tier 1 decks from Pro Tour Wherever".

The reason kitchen table Magic works, and why it's still the most "pure" form of Magic, is simple. Largely, the people playing it are either ignorant of the meta or choose to intentionally ignore it. These players don't know that an objectively better version of their favorite 6 mana sorcery released in Aetherdrift as a mythic rare. They just play the card because it works in their deck and they like it. They don't know that the surveil lands from Murders at Karlov Manor are amazing cards with a hefty price tag. They sit in a box in the closet because they enter tapped. _"That sucks, and why would I want to put a card in my graveyard on purpose?"_

Unfortunately, these players are becoming extinct. It's hard not to break out of that innocence when there is so much Magic content and so many products being made. A simple Google search can shatter that glass permanently. Not to mention that if a player ends up really liking the game, they _want_ to learn more. They decide to watch that highly-polished Game Knights video with 5 million views (sponsored by Wizards of the Coast), or the interesting-sounding Tolarian Community College video with the funny Micro Center ad. Before they know it, they've entered the micro-culture and begin to absorb it... to become part of it. If you've never watched the Folding Ideas video, [Why It's Rude to Suck at Warcraft](https://youtu.be/BKP1I7IocYU?si=BkblQaInPNyIwB7C), you should. It perfectly describes the environment created by **the meta**. It's as true for Magic as it is for World of Warcraft. The more connected we become, the harder it is to avoid it.

But, I would wager that for many people, those halcyon days were their favorite days playing Magic. Before they were concerned with the meta, before they knew about [EDHRec](https://edhrec.com/), before they knew how to buy singles instead of cracking packs, they were just playing the game because it was fun to slap down a cardboard monster with pretty art. It was entertaining to imagine you were a super-powerful wizard throwing lightning bolts and summoning dragons.

## Magic Has Become Commander

Wizards of the Coast has a financial incentive to sell as many cards to as many people as possible. This means it's only natural for them to target the casual players more than the established ones; to appeal to the masses and hope to get them hooked on their definitely-not-gambling scheme. It's why so much of Magic is now _Universes Beyond_ - crossovers with popular third-party IPs like Final Fantasy or Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. These are recognizable brands that have an audience outside of Magic's typical sphere. Millions of people that have played Final Fantasy have probably never tried Magic, but they might if their favorite characters are on the cards.

These casual players will never go straight into sweating it up at their local game store, playing in 1v1 Standard tournaments. No, WotC realized that the casual nature of Commander was perfect as an avenue to expand their reach while still providing a _mostly_ structured play environment. They can continue printing powerful Commander cards (remember [Nadu](https://scryfall.com/card/mh3/193/nadu-winged-wisdom)?) in new sets to keep demand high with entrenched players _**and also**_ attract the sort of crowd who plays a Spider-Man deck because they really like Spider-Man, whether it can win the game or not. It's a win/win for them.

In fact, in many ways, Magic has become Commander and vice versa. While Standard and the other 1v1 competitive formats still exist and enjoy success, Commander is now the default way to play. Just like the kitchen table Magic of old, right?

## Game Engine Friction

Remember how I said earlier that my parents and I had an understanding back in the 90s? We played Magic to win. That quaint combination of being both **a)** naive enough to build dumpster decks based on simple themes and also **b)** competitive enough to play primarily to win... that's a hard-to-maintain innocence. Like I said earlier, once that glass shatters, you can't put it back together. That innate understanding, though, didn't spawn from that naivety.

You might imagine we were just hyper-competitive. That's certainly true to some degree. My dad was always ruthless in every video game, board game, or card game we played. He liked to win, and my mom hated to lose. **But that's not it either**.

No, I want to delve deeper. I'm going to propose that Magic's thematic design and game engine are inherently aggressive and competitive. The pillars of its design are built with a cut-throat mindset. The game only has one true victory condition: you must defeat your opponents to win. The only objective of the game is to win.

### Thematic Competition

Even thematically, from its very inception, Magic was built around 1v1 competition. It's all about two powerful wizards slinging spells and creatures at each other until one of them perishes. You can feel it in the vibes of early sets. It's evident in the way the rulebooks presented the game and its world. The focus on the two brothers, Urza vs. Mishra battling it out eternally, was as surrogate for you vs. your opponent(s).

As the game grew beyond generic theme sets like _Arabian Nights_ and into what would eventually become an ongoing plot, these thematic through-lines diminished, but it took several years for them to disappear entirely. Even with the introduction of Planeswalker cards in 2007's _Lorwyn_, Magic's designers were very careful to preserve the game's core thematic conceit. You, the player, are still a powerful Planeswalker. You're just summoning these other Planeswalkers as temporary allies. That's why a Planeswalker's counters are called _**loyalty counters**_; because after getting damaged enough or doing enough for you, they're no longer loyal enough to stick around. They don't die, they just leave.

### Mechanical Aggression

Beyond the fantasy flavor of spell-slinging Planeswalkers, though, lies a game engine whose design pillars are inherently competitive. Even during its earliest iterations, this mindset was influencing the mechanics. Let's break down a couple examples.

#### Instants

Magic has always been a game about interaction between players. Naturally, 30-something years of design iteration and player skill improvement has turned that interaction into a well-understood economy, but instants (and the now-defunct interrupts) were present from the very beginning.

The equivalent of Magic's instant-speed actions aren't actually all that common in games. Think about most other games you've played: card games, tabletop games, board games, and even most competitive video games that aren't real-time simulations. Sometimes, it will permit you a very limited subset of "reactions" you can take based on triggers. Those triggers are either face-up, or (as in Yu-Gi-Oh) at least known to exist. Very rarely, there will be action cards that can be played "at any time" and affect the game state immediately, but these are often heavily restricted. The game may only feature a couple of them in the entire deck.

Magic takes this idea much further. Certain decks profit immensely from acting almost entirely on _another player's turn_. You're encouraged to do this, because you shorten the window of time during which your resources (mana, creatures, etc.) are expended. The game design begs you to wait until the last possible second to make your play; you put yourself at a disadvantage otherwise. As a competitive player, it can feel great to master this! You've minimized your weaknesses and maximized your strengths, giving you a leg up on your opponent. 

If you're both playing at that level of skill, a whole new level of intrigue develops. Now you're not playing cards anymore so much as you're playing each other. An entire language develops around these mind games - see the phrase _"you've got to make them have it"_, referring to the idea that you can't play the game in fear of what your opponent _might_ have in their hand. Instead, you have to make calculated bets based on your understanding of the **metagame** (what their deck might contain), **public knowledge** (cards in the graveyard or exile they've already spent), and that player's **disposition** (how they might act or react).

Making the most of this, however, necessitates a pretty deep understanding of the rules. When can I play instants? What is the stack? When do triggered abilities go on the stack? What is priority? Who has it right now? Why can't I stop this creature's triggered ability from firing when it enters the battlefield? Why can't I keep my opponent from activating their Planeswalker's +1 ability? Why can't I respond to them tapping their lands to get ready to cast a spell?

The understanding and proper usage of instant-speed interaction is probably one of the biggest differentiators between a novice and an expert player. Casual players are going to get very frustrated when they try to play the game and you take advantage of these rules to shut them down. Countering their spells is one thing, but how about casting Lightning Bolt on a creature they've targeted with an Aura? This is known as a "two-for-one" - your **one** spell cost your opponent **two** game objects. How could they even know that was something they should watch for? Why even play these cards if the opponent can (hopefully metaphorically) slap them out of your hand and laugh at you?

Compare Magic in this way to Hearthstone, Blizzard's Warcraft-themed virtual trading card game. Now, a disclaimer: I haven't played it in a _long time_ so I can't be sure this still holds true. However, while Magic and Hearthstone are superficially similar, their design pillars are very different. There is no instant-speed interaction in Hearthstone. It's played entirely "on the board" so to speak; that is, game pieces which can interact with the game state are laid out onto the playfield and visible to both players. This doesn't necessarily make it less of a tactical or strategic game, but it does absolutely change the feeling of playing it. Its design isn't as _aggressive_ as Magic's.

#### Lands

Magic's design also takes a very cavalier attitude toward players getting completely shut out of the game by randomness. It goes without saying <small>(but I'm going to say it anyway...)</small> that randomness is a huge part of most games, especially card games. Part of the appeal is that no two games are likely to play out exactly the same. However, in Magic, the peaks and valleys of this randomness are _steep_. This high variance, high impact facet of its design contributes to its inherent competitiveness. You might argue otherwise - that a competitive game should strive to be more fair - but that's the thing. It views fairness in the most absolute way. All players are subject to the same conditions, and have the same responsibility to mitigate their risk.

One of the most common and painful manifestations of this factor is getting a poor distribution of lands during the game. Called either "mana screw" or "mana flood" (too few or too many lands, respectively), a player can randomly be left unable to play effectively. Putting aside a few edge cases, lands are vital to a player's ability to play their cards. Almost every card in the game has a mana cost, and activated abilities often have mana costs too. So, you need a steady flow of mana to keep playing cards and taking game actions. Additionally, because the game is designed around you continuing to draw lands as the game progresses, your game-ending threats tend to cost more mana. You need to have enough mana later in the game to cast your win conditions. Not having enough of it, when you need it, leaves you sitting with a hand full of fun things you can't afford to do.

On the flip side, you also need to draw cards that enable you to take impactful game actions. Very few decks can win the game after drawing lands for four turns in a row. They just... run out of stuff to do. The spectrum between mana screw and mana flood is where a majority of matches operate, and I'd wager most have a healthy variance here which, on average, creates exciting gameplay for all parties. But, Magic is happy to let you have a very bad time. A player is supposed to account for these factors during deck-building, and that's a great mechanic! Learning to account for the "mana curve" of your deck (the distribution of casting costs vs. number of lands) and doing the math around tweaking your chance to cast your critical spells on time is another mark of an expert player. But even the best players can only mitigate so much risk. They get mana flooded and screwed too, just maybe less frequently than a novice.

Thankfully, the game does provide one built-in mechanic to help dodge these screw/flood situations after deck-building. A player can choose to mulligan their starting hand before the game begins. However, this still puts the player at a measurable, objective disadvantage due solely to bad luck. You don't get to draw a new, full hand of 7 cards. You have to pitch one of your new cards to the bottom of your library. Your opponent has 7 game pieces but now you only have 6. Still screwed after the mulligan? You can do it again... now you're down to 5 cards, and so on.

Entrenched Magic players will regale you with stories about the times they've won games after taking a deep mulligan, and those are valid and exciting moments worthy of memorializing. It's also not a good idea to let players take infinite mulligans to get _just the right hand_, because that disproportionately favors combo decks who could stack their opening hand perfectly to win quickly. The point here isn't necessarily to poke holes in the rules. It's to demonstrate that these design pillars are aggressive and competitive by nature.

#### Aggression

Magic's design is full of interactions like this. These are important mechanics that are coded as inherently _aggressive_ but are absolutely part of proficient play. This is part of why Magic's design pillars are highly competitive and cut-throat. The game wants you to play it like this, and will punish you if you don't.

## It's You. You're The Problem

We've established that Magic has several core design elements which encourage highly competitive and aggressive play. It's that subconscious suggestion that made my parents and I understand, back in the 90s, that Magic was a game meant to be played to win.

So why, then, is so much of the discourse around Commander so confused? Why is the Commander Bracket System so concerned with the "feeling" of the game and players' expectations? Why do people talk so much about letting players have extra mulligans, or making house rules where you can take free mulligans if you have less than 3 lands in your opening hand? Why is the metagame of Commander so concerned with the **_experience of playing it_**?

Well... it's because of you. You can't agree on what the spirit of Commander is supposed to be.

### Too Many Things to Too Many People

Commander is a _divisive_ format, despite being the de facto way to play Magic. When you sit down to play a game of Standard with a single opponent at FNM, you're both entering that match with the understanding that you're playing a game, but you're going to try and win that game. That's why you're playing. Commander tries to strike an uneasy balance between that motivation - to win - and making "having fun" an explicit goal rather than an intrinsic one.

Instead of you and one opponent, you've got four people tied up in this game. Turns take longer because each player has four boards worth of game state to manage instead of two - an exponential increase in complexity, rather than linear. Decks are bigger and life pools are larger. Inevitably, **playing Commander becomes more of a social experience**. You chat with the other players while someone is searching their library, or trying to reason about their hand, or resolving a complex stack. You're encouraged to begin every game with a conversation, that aforementioned Rule Zero conversation, wherein you're supposed to open up about your deck and its game plan a little. Really, any time you sit down with multiple other people at a table to play a game, you're more likely to treat it as a social gathering. Just ask any tabletop RPG group whose players spend a large chunk of the playtime gabbing about their day.

But things get messy once the game actually begins. The reason Wizards of the Coast created the bracket system, the reason why that Rule Zero conversation became crystallized, and the cause of all the confusion is because people want an array of different things out of their social gaming experiences.

The bracket system has a place for people who play Commander to win. It's bracket 5, called CEDH (the `C` stands for competitive, I guess). CEDH is a long-standing variant of Commander/EDH wherein the only major difference in the mindset the players bring. They elect to play Commander the same way every other Magic format is played. They use the best cards they can, follow the metagame, and play following the spirit of the rules.

The existence of Competitive EDH implies that "normal" Commander isn't meant to be as competitive. Commander has taken on a life, _for some players_, as a place to goof around with the Magic game engine. Even that, though, falls on a spectrum of severity. Again, you see this in the codified guidelines of the bracket system - it tries to distill that spectrum into discrete boundaries. Bracket 4? You're using all the best cards, probably, but not paying too much attention to the metagame. Bracket 3? You want to play to win but only with things you find fun, and without necessarily breaking the bank. Brackets 1 and 2? You're just happy to be playing Magic and slapping cardboard on the table. That's not to imply you won't maybe try to win the game, but you're not too bothered about it.

Maybe you disagree with my assessments of the individual brackets, and that's fine. It's hard to distill a spectrum of so many different expectations into only five groups. In fact, that's my point. You don't agree with me on what Commander is supposed to be. These sorts of disagreements form the foundation of the constantly churning discourse around the format.

### Friction Burns

This is where that _competitive_ and _aggressive_ nature inherent to Magic comes to a head. The very game engine itself is nudging you to play a certain way, but that way isn't necessarily conducive to a fun social experience. It's not _fun_, strictly speaking, to get two-for-one'd by instant speed removal. Getting bored while a combo player meticulously demonstrates their winning infinite loop isn't going to brighten up your Friday night. The best social games are designed with _wildly_ different mentalities. Their rules and interactions are built in ways that encourage fun interaction with other players. Magic is... very much not. It's designed for you to build a deck that wins the game, and then to take advantage of its entire toolset to do so.

### The Spirit of Commander

The irony of all this is everyone knows Commander is a flawed format. Even its creators willingly acknowledged this. It was described as a way to have some fun between more traditional 1v1 matches. I won't imply that folks like Sheldon Menery (the "father" of EDH) didn't put a lot of love and care into its development. They absolutely did. But I do strongly believe that it was never meant to grow as big as it has, nor was it meant to be the _default format_ of Magic.

It's not unreasonable, then, to say that the spirit of Commander is whatever you want it to be. Clearly, Magic itself is a fun enough game - and a timeless enough design - that despite all this friction, dissonance, and discourse around Commander, people just want to play the damn game. Getting to play with more people? That's a net positive, even if it's hard to nail down exactly what each player is looking to get out of it.

But the growing pains associated with the emphasis and codification Wizards has been placing on it? To me, that is one of the only things that _actually_ goes against the spirit of Commander.

## More Meta, More Problems

As I alluded to earlier, Magic is Commander and Commander is Magic. Wizards is highly invested in making sure it's successful, and like centralized governing bodies with a financial interest are wont to do, they have been putting structure around it so they can protect it. But EDH doesn't feel like it was ever meant to be structured. Its charm was in its lack of structure, inherently - a return to the kitchen table slop decks of the past. A way to play with cards we already had...

...oh. Yeah, I guess that's kind of a problem for WotC, huh?

I don't even know if the designers or bean counters at the company are doing this intentionally. I think it's probably just emergent behavior created from this sort of corporate environment. But it's hard to deny how _convenient_ it is that their motives explicitly need you to buy more cards all the time. No one's going to blame them for printing attractive cards in new sets; that's their business model and **I want the designers and artists to get paid**. But as we established earlier, kitchen table players don't really pay attention to the meta. They're not going to know that a better card exists unless they happen to open a pack that has one in it.

So, Wizards is incentivized to do the following:

1. Create a welcoming environment that encourages new/casual players to join the meta through official events at your LGS (and, in the modern day, sponsored YouTube videos showing friendly high-level Commander play).
2. Establish a structured play environment where the meta is stratified into tiers within which players can choose their comfort level.
3. Allow that meta to form, let these players participate, and inevitably get defeated by others who min/max within it, no matter the bracket.
4. Sell players better cards to improve their decks and compete within the meta.

There's not really anything wrong with any of this. It is, after all, the same way other Magic formats have worked since the creation of Type 2 in 1995. And hey, if you're having fun, then it's no more harmful than any other hobby. But, arguably, it's not at all what EDH was meant to be. You can make the same argument about the creation of Commander-exclusive products and cards legal only in Commander <small>(and Legacy/Vintage, by happenstance)</small>. 

If the original intent of EDH was for it to be a casual format of slapped-together decks made from cards we already had, to have some less-competitive fun within a highly-competitive game, it's clearly deviated from that intent. Today, it's closer than ever before to the other main Magic formats. It's more akin to Modern now: it's got an expensive meta, its own dedicated product lines, a cycle of _soft_ rotation where new cards keep power-creeping out old established staples, and a fervent community of players constantly discussing and picking it apart. And the existence of sites like EDHRec only further consolidates these factors. <small>(No hate to them. Someone was going to make a tool like it, and they seem like cool people.)</small>

Reiterating a point from earlier, some amount of this crystallization and codification was inevitable. **Everything** has a meta nowadays. But it seems to me that the only thing Commander has left going for it to differentiate it spiritually from other formats - the social factor - is starting to fray. And it's fraying because WotC can't stop sticking their fingers in the pie. Their need to protect and standardize their primary money-making format is going to inevitably smother the one thing which makes it so much different.

## Full Circle

I told you the story of how I got into Magic and how I played it for over a decade, in the 90s and early 2000s. I engaged with it the way the rules encouraged me: to win. When I came back in 2022, I was met with a landscape _very different_ from the one I left. It was one dominated by a much more friendly and social game than the sweaty, smelly, try-hard tournaments I had burned out on. But even since then, I've watched as this version of the game has begun to twist back into the meta-dominated competition it once was.

I truly believe some of that decay is because of Magic's game engine. It's inherently aggressive - my wife has called it "boy-coded".
